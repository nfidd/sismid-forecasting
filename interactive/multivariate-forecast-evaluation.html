Scoring joint forecasts: the energy score

When you forecast several places at once, the marginal forecasts can be identical yet the joint forecast — how the places move together — can differ completely. The energy score is a proper scoring rule that sees this.

Marginal is not joint

Same one-location forecasts, different picture of what happens together.

Suppose you forecast influenza activity in two regions, A and B. Your forecast is not two separate numbers but a cloud of plausible pairs (A, B) — a joint distribution. Two forecasts can have exactly the same marginal distribution in A and the same marginal in B, and still disagree about whether A and B tend to rise together.

Averaging a one-location score such as the CRPS over regions only ever sees the marginals. It cannot tell these forecasts apart. But many decisions depend on the joint distribution: if you hold a fixed stockpile to split across regions, a peak that hits everywhere at once is a very different problem from peaks that arrive one region at a time. To score that, you need a rule that reads the whole vector at once.

The energy score score · lower is better

A multivariate generalisation of the CRPS.

The energy score takes a forecast that is a distribution F over a vector outcome (here the pair (A, B)) and the observed vector y, and returns one number. Written with draws X, X′ from the forecast and the Euclidean distance ‖·‖:

ES(F, y) = E‖X − y‖ − ½ E‖X − X′‖

It is exactly the "energy" form of the CRPS with absolute value replaced by vector distance: the first term rewards accuracy (draws close to the truth), the second rewards sharpness (a concentrated cloud). In one dimension it is the CRPS.

Why the two panels disagree

The marginal CRPS for A depends only on region A's forecast and region A's outcome; likewise for B. Rotating the cloud leaves both marginals untouched, so their average is frozen. The energy score reads the pair together: when the observation sits against the cloud's tilt — say both regions high while the forecast expected a trade-off — it pays a penalty the marginals never charge.

Things worth remembering

  • The energy score is proper for the whole joint distribution and reduces to the CRPS in one dimension, so it is a genuine generalisation.
  • It is reported in the units of the data and, like the CRPS, is best read by comparing forecasts rather than in isolation.
  • You need joint samples to compute it. Fitting each region on its own gives independent draws (ρ = 0); capturing dependence needs a genuinely joint model (e.g. a vector autoregression).
  • It is known to be relatively insensitive to misspecified dependence in high dimensions — a reason people also turn to the variogram score, or to modelling the scores directly.
